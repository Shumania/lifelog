The world

Cumbia is a seventy-year migration more than a genre. It starts as gaita-flute-and-tambora village music on Colombia's Caribbean coast (San Jacinto, Sampués), gets urbanised by Lucho Bermúdez's 1950s big band and then industrialised by Medellín's Discos Fuentes label in the 1960s — accordion, guacharaca, the Corraleros house band, Andrés Landero — whose Sonora Dinamita carried it to Mexico in the late '70s. In Peru it mutated into chicha: Enrique Delgado's surf-guitar cumbia (Lima, 1968), Juaneco's ayahuasca-tinged Pucallpa combo and Los Mirlos' Amazonian birdsong, then Los Shapis' working-class Andean chicha of the '80s. Mexico and Texas made it pop — Selena's Tejano cumbia, Iztapalapa's Los Ángeles Azules, Celso Piña fusing Monterrey accordion with Control Machete's hip-hop — and a 2008–2020 revival (Brooklyn's Barbès, Bogotá's Ondatrópica and Meridian Brothers, Buenos Aires' ZZK, Mexico City's Sonido Gallo Negro) handed the sound to Los Angeles, where LA LOM — three LA natives who started in 2021 as the Hollywood Roosevelt Hotel lobby band and name their tunes after the city's streets and neighbourhoods — made the instrumental version that Andrew keeps returning to.

Neighbours, for the record: Bosphorus Fuzz (Aug 31) was the Khruangbin / BALTHVS / Glass Beams / Hermanos Gutiérrez instrumental-groove world that LA LOM gets filed next to — this night is deliberately the sung lineage behind it, LA LOM the only door. Las Caras Lindas (Sep 30, salsa) and Discovery card #280 (dancehall/reggaetón) are adjacent Latin worlds; cumbia is neither.

Selection method

Lineage adjacency, chapter by chapter — every ring artist is a documented link in the chain (label, city, or a direct cover/collaboration), never a similar-artists hop:

  • Root / Discos Fuentes: Los Gaiteros de San Jacinto (the gaita source — Smithsonian Folkways 2006, Latin Grammy 2007) → Lucho Bermúdez with Matilde Díaz (Prende la Vela, RCA Victor 1952 — the big-band urbanisation) → Los Corraleros de Majagual with Eliseo Herrera (La Burrita, Fuentes' house band, 1967) → Andrés Landero (La Pava Congona, 1972, San Jacinto's accordion king) → La Sonora Dinamita with Lucho Argaín (Se Me Perdió la Cadenita, 1978 — the record that conquered Mexico).
  • Peru / chicha: Los Destellos (A Patricia, 1968 — Delgado's founding electric-guitar cumbia; the ID is the 1971 Constelación catalogue release) → Juaneco y Su Combo (Mujer Hilandera, Pucallpa 1972, Infopesa) → Los Mirlos (La Danza de los Mirlos, Infopesa 7", 1973) → Los Shapis (El Aguajal, Los Auténticos 1981).
  • Texas / Mexico: Selena (Baila Esta Cumbia, Ven Conmigo 1990, written by A.B. Quintanilla and Pete Astudillo) → Los Ángeles Azules (Cómo Te Voy a Olvidar, Inolvidables 1996, Iztapalapa) → Celso Piña with Control Machete and Blanquito Man (Cumbia sobre el río, Barrio Bravo 2001, Monterrey).
  • Revival: Chicha Libre (Sonido Amazónico, Barbès 2008 — Olivier Conan's Brooklyn band covering the Mirlos tune, so Los Mirlos themselves appear only with La Danza) → Ondatrópica (Tiene Sabor, Tiene Sazón, 2012 — Mario Galeano and Quantic recording Fuentes veterans in the Fuentes studio) → La Yegros (Viene de Mí, ZZK 2013, produced by King Coya) → Meridian Brothers (Cumbia del Pichamán, Cumbia Siglo XXI, Bongo Joe 2020) → Sonido Gallo Negro (Bocanegra, Cumbia Salvaje 2012, Mexico City's instrumental chicha).
  • Door: LA LOM ×4 — Santee Alley (2022) and three cuts from The Los Angeles League of Musicians (Verve, 2024).

Connective tissue & sequencing

The through-line is the thing the playlist is named for: the guacharaca scraper and the two-step caminadito bass that survive every mutation — gaita to accordion to surf guitar to Casio to a hip-hop beat to LA LOM's hollow-body. The night opens on Santee Alley because that is the door Andrew walked through this week (a downtown-LA alley named in the title — the destination stated first), then jumps back to the root and walks forward: Colombia (gaita → big band → accordion → the Dinamita hit that leaves for Mexico), Peru (Delgado's guitar hinge → the Amazon → Lima's chicha), north through Texas, Iztapalapa and Monterrey, then the revival from Brooklyn, Bogotá and Buenos Aires. Sonido Gallo Negro (2012) sits after Meridian Brothers (2020) on purpose — geography over strict chronology: Mexico City's instrumental chicha is the direct hand-off to LA LOM's three-song close (Angels Point → Lorena → Rebecca). It closes on Rebecca because it is the most-played LA LOM track on both stores — the lineage ends where the habit already is. ~78 minutes; high energy is honest for a dance music, mixed vocals because the Spanish-sung ring (14 cuts) frames seven instrumentals (the door plus A Patricia, La Danza de los Mirlos, Bocanegra).
