The world

Tokyo, 1969 to 1978, and mostly one ward of it. Angura (the Japanese contraction of "underground") was first a theatre movement: Terayama Shūji's troupe Tenjō Sajiki, the basement stage at Sasori-za, the playwright Betsuyaku Minoru. It produced singers almost by accident. Terayama staged Maki Asakawa's first recitals and wrote her lyrics; Carmen Maki was a teenager in his company when he wrote her the hit that put her on the 1969 Kōhaku broadcast. Around them the same streets had Keiko Fuji's back-alley enka (her debut is literally called "Woman of Shinjuku"), the student movement's songs, the gekiga comics of the day, and Nikkatsu's youth films. Producer Teramoto Yukiji built a small stable around Asakawa (Itsurō Shimoda, Masato Minami, Lily), and by 1975 a second generation of dark-folk singers (Morita Dōji, Hako Yamasaki, Kazuki Tomokawa) had inherited the black dress and the small room.

Neighbour, for the record: Hachikō Exit (Oct 4) was Shibuya-kei, Tokyo 1988–2001, bright pop made from record collections. This is a different station two decades earlier, and no artist is shared.

Selection method

Adjacency by documented link to Asakawa's circle (who wrote for whom, who produced whom, who shared a stage, film or label), never similar-artists. Each link below was checked against Japanese-language sources (Wikipedia JA, label pages, Discogs, the producer's own interviews) before the build:

  • Terayama / Tenjō Sajiki: Carmen Maki (company member; lyric by Terayama, music by Tanaka Michi, released Feb 21, 1969) · Asakawa's かもめ (lyric Terayama, music Yamaki Kōzaburō, B-side of her July 1, 1969 debut single) · 眠るのが怖い (lyric Terayama, music Itsurō Shimoda; Asakawa singing it unaccompanied, from the 2015 archive collection of Tenjō Sajiki stage and film music) · Kan Mikami (from Aomori like Terayama, and in the cast of Terayama's 1974 film Den-en ni Shisu).
  • Teramoto Yukiji's stable: he found Asakawa with Terayama in 1968 and later produced Shimoda, Masato Minami and Lily. Asakawa also took Minami's song あたしのブギウギ into her own repertoire, and Lily sings on the 1973 Minami album used here.
  • Shinjuku and 1969: Keiko Fuji's debut single 新宿の女 (Sep 25, 1969) · Tokiko Kato's ひとり寝の子守唄 (Sep 1969, written out of letters from the jailed student leader Fujimoto Toshio, whom she married in prison in 1972) · Yoshio Hayakawa's サルビアの花 (1969; he fronted Jacks, who scored Wakamatsu Kōji's 1968 film Haragashi Onna, and was a director at the URC label) · Hitoshi Komuro's 雨が空から降れば (1971, lyric by the playwright Betsuyaku Minoru).
  • Print and screen: Morio Agata's 赤色エレジー (1972, Bellwood's first single, after Hayashi Seiichi's gekiga of the same name; the album take with Hachimitsu Pie is used) · Akiyuki Nosaka, the novelist, singing 黒の舟唄 (1971) · Seri Ishikawa's 八月の濡れた砂 (theme of Fujita Toshiya's 1971 Nikkatsu film) · Meiko Kaji's 修羅の花 (theme of Lady Snowblood, 1973, also directed by Fujita).
  • The second wave: Morita Dōji's debut single (Oct 1975; her last concert was at Shinjuku Loft in 1983) · Hako Yamasaki's 望郷 from her debut Tobimasu (Elec, Oct 1, 1975) · Kazuki Tomokawa's サーカス (1978, a Nakahara Chūya poem, arranged by J.A. Seazer, Tenjō Sajiki's house composer) · Naomi Chiaki's 夜へ急ぐ人 (Sep 1977, words and music by Tomokawa; she also sang Asakawa's Japanese lyric for "House of the Rising Sun").

The weakest link is Hako Yamasaki: I found no shared collaborator, only the same year, the same register and the same shelf in every account of the period. She is here on those grounds and no others.

Connective tissue & sequencing

  • Opens on 夜が明けたら, her first single and her own song: when the night ends she will take the first train out. Everything after it is the night.
  • Terayama's two Makis (1–3): Asakawa, then Carmen Maki's motherless-child lullaby, then かもめ. Two Terayama lyrics back to back, both from 1969.
  • Shinjuku 1969 (4–6): Fuji's bar hostess, Kato's cell lullaby, Hayakawa's piano and one voice. Three records from the same year.
  • Stage and page (7–10): Betsuyaku's rain song as a two-minute breath, then Mikami's own words to 夢は夜ひらく (his version was once barred from broadcast), Nosaka's river between men and women, and Agata's lament for a comic strip.
  • Screen (11–12): two Fujita Toshiya films, a summer beach and a snowfall. Kaji is the one voice here the library already knew.
  • The producer's room (13–16): Asakawa alone with Shimoda's tune, Minami's ten-to-four-in-the-morning (Caramel Mama is the band, and reviews call it the funkiest cut on his album; it sits where the night is deepest), Asakawa's こんな風に過ぎて行くのなら, then Lily's husky voice. All four are Teramoto's artists.
  • 1975 and after (17–20): Morita's farewell to a friend, Yamasaki's homesickness, Tomokawa's circus, and Chiaki taking Tomokawa's song onto national television.
  • Closes on それはスポットライトではない, first released on 灯ともし頃 (1976), an album recorded at the Aketa no Mise jazz room with Ryūichi Sakamoto among the players. The light she is singing about is not the stage light. It is the longest track and the last word.
