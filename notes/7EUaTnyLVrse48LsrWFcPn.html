The world

The rock canon from 1964 to 1968, as it actually happened on 45s: the British R&B boom (Them, Pretty Things, Yardbirds, Animals, Who) hitting the American charts, and American teenagers in garages answering back with fuzz pedals and one-chord snarls (Sonics, Seeds, Standells, Count Five, Elevators). By 1966–67 the two lines braid into psychedelia — the Byrds' raga guitar, the Move and Small Faces on the Marquee-club side, Love and Moby Grape on the Sunset Strip/Fillmore side — and by 1968 it's turned heavy (Hendrix, Big Brother, Blue Cheer). This is the whole syllabus for what "classic rock" means; Andrew has heard the Stones, Doors and Dead chapters many times over. These are the other chapters.

Selection method

Every track is a period single or lead album cut that appears on the standard canon lists (Nuggets, the Rolling Stone 500, Christgau's 60s consumer guide) — corroborated, not vibed. Adjacency is by scene and lineage: the Marquee/Ealing R&B club circuit (Who → Yardbirds → Creation → Small Faces → Move), Los Angeles garage-into-psych (Seeds → Love → Byrds → Electric Prunes), Texas/Northwest garage (Elevators, Sonics), Bay Area (Moby Grape, Big Brother, Blue Cheer). Heard-vs-new verified by bare track ID pre-build: 25 of 25 new; artist-level exposure zero for every act except the Kinks, Love, Byrds-adjacent Buffalo Springfield and Hendrix — and for those four the chosen track had never played. Cream ("Sunshine of Your Love") and Steppenwolf ("Magic Carpet Ride") were cut: only compilation masters on Spotify and both are songs Andrew has certainly heard in life even if not in the log. Moby Grape's debut LP isn't on Spotify at all; "Omaha" is the same 1967 Columbia master on a compilation.

Connective tissue & sequencing

  • 1964–65, British beat (1–6): "Gloria" (Them) is the three-chord Big Bang; "She's Not There" answers with minor-key cool; the Pretty Things and the Who are the Marquee scene going feral; the Yardbirds add the Eastern drone; the Animals close the chapter with the working-class hymn.
  • American garage (7–13): Tacoma's "Strychnine" is the pivot to the US — everything from here to "Making Time" is the Nuggets spine: Seeds, Standells, Count Five, the Elevators' electric jug, Love's "Seven and Seven Is" (proto-punk with a nuclear ending), then the Creation's violin-bow guitar to swing back to London.
  • 1966–67, the braid (14–20): Spencer Davis's Hammond stomp → the Byrds go raga → the Electric Prunes' backwards guitars → the Move and Small Faces (British psych-pop at full color) → "Waterloo Sunset," the one deliberate breather, placed two-thirds through → "Mr. Soul," Neil Young reworking "Satisfaction" as menace.
  • 1967–68, heavy (21–25): "Fire," "Omaha," "Piece of My Heart," Nazz, and Blue Cheer's "Summertime Blues" — the loudest record of 1968 and the door into the '70s.
  • Opens on "Gloria" because every garage band on the list learned it first. Closes on Blue Cheer because that's where the 60s stop being the 60s.
