The world

Same country, same instruments, the other rooms. The first Gut Strings stood in the 1973 founding institutions — Hogwood, Pinnock, Phillips, Rooley, Gardiner — and their heirs. Around them the British revival had a pre-history and a hinterland: Alfred Deller, the countertenor who sang Purcell and Dowland in the 1940s before anyone had a period band, and Julian Bream, who taught himself the lute and recorded Dowland in a Wiltshire chapel in 1967; Andrew Parrott's Taverner Consort, the fourth 1973 foundation and the one Vol. 1 skipped; the medievalists of the 1970s–80s — the Hilliard Ensemble (1974), Christopher Page's Gothic Voices (1980), whose 1981 Hildegard record became Hyperion's best-seller, the Dufay Collective and the Orlando Consort (late 1980s); the viol consorts that followed Fretwork — the Rose Consort (1984), Laurence Dreyfus's Phantasm (1994); the regional bands that dug up their own local baroque — the Avison Ensemble in Newcastle, Concerto Caledonia in Glasgow, Ex Cathedra in Birmingham reaching all the way to colonial Bolivia — and the post-2000 generation (Alamire, the Brook Street Band, La Nuova Musica, the Cardinall's Musick's Byrd Edition). The repertoire this time runs six centuries, Hildegard to Arne; the sound is still gut, viols, cornetts and sagbutts, voices one to a part.

Selection method

Adjacency by institution and lineage again, never Spotify-similar: every performer founded, directed, or came up through a British early-music ensemble (the only non-British repertoire — Pérotin, Machaut, Striggio, Praetorius, Matteis, Araujo, Pergolesi — arrives through British bands, exactly as Corelli and Vivaldi did in Vol. 1). Three constraints on top of Vol. 1's: no ensemble that appeared in Vol. 1, with two deliberate exceptions — the Taverner Consort/Players as this list's spine (twice: Tallis 1985, Purcell 1989) and Emma Kirkby, who sang Dowland for Rooley on the first list and here opens (Hildegard, 1981) and closes (Arne) — a voice-bookend across forty years of records; no work from Vol. 1 (Dowland returns, but Essex Galliard and In darkness, not Lachrimae); and chronology by composition, not recording, so the music itself does the walking, 1150 → 1760. One work per entry, 22 distinct works, 21 ensembles. Heard-vs-new verified pre-build by bare track ID against consumption_history + listening_history + liked_tracks: 21 of 22 new (anchor = Phantasm's Lawes Fantazy, Aug 1 2026); by work, 18 of 22 are new — Spem, Beata viscera and the Stabat Mater are known pieces in recordings Andrew hasn't heard, and are labelled so above. Recording dates checked where Spotify's tag is a reissue: Bream's Dances of Dowland was recorded in 1967 (Wardour Chapel), Gothic Voices' Hildegard in 1981, Parrott's Tallis in 1985, Phantasm's six-part Lawes in 2002 (Channel Classics). Nothing from the warhorse shelf — no Canon, no Four Seasons, no Dido's Lament; Spem is the one famous piece, kept because it is the hinge of the whole English story (see below).

Connective tissue & sequencing

Chronological by composition, in five rooms:

  • Before England had a baroque (1–5): Kirkby's Columba aspexit with Hildegard's symphonia, the record that put Gothic Voices on the map → the Hilliard's Pérotin, the ECM disc that made four English voices a world act → the Dufay Collective's estampie (the one purely instrumental medieval track: fiddle, pipe and drum) → the Orlando Consort's Machaut, a garden song from their Hyperion years → Alamire's Agincourt Carol, 1415, the first English voice in the set.
  • The forty-part hinge (6–7): Striggio's Ecce beatam lucem for 40 voices was carried to London in 1567 (Striggio visited the English court that year); Tallis's Spem in alium, also in 40 parts, is generally read as England's answer. I Fagiolini's 2011 reconstruction first, Parrott's 1985 Spem second — the Italian provocation and the English reply, back to back, in the one place on the list where two giants sit side by side.
  • Elizabethan and Jacobean (8–12): Byrd in English (the Cardinall's Musick, from the 1611 Psalmes, Songs and Sonnets) → Dowland twice from the pre-history — Bream's galliard (1967), then Deller, in his last years, singing In darkness let me dwell with Robert Spencer (1977) — the two men who were doing this before the revival had a name → the Rose Consort's Gibbons pavan → Pickett's Praetorius Voltes, the 1986 Oiseau-Lyre record, to turn the lights up.
  • The Stuart court and after (13–17): Phantasm's Lawes Fantazy, the anchor, consort music for Charles I → Locke's suite for the sagbutts and cornetts of Charles II's coronation year, played by the band named after them → Matteis, the Neapolitan who arrived in London around 1670 and taught the English to listen to a violin (Palladian Ensemble, 1993) → Purcell's Three Parts upon a Ground, three violins over one bass line, Parrott's Taverner Players with Holloway, Wallfisch and Bury → Ex Cathedra's Araujo, a Christmas negrilla from 1690s La Plata — the British revival's longest reach, dropped in as the wild card.
  • Georgian Britain and the coda (18–22): Handel's trio sonata (the Brook Street Band, named for his house) → Pergolesi's Stabat Mater opening, Crowe and Mead with La Nuova Musica — the one hush in this room → Avison's Scarlatti concerto, Newcastle 1744, the provincial English baroque Vol. 1 reached through Barsanti's Edinburgh → the Earl of Kellie, Edinburgh's own "Fiddler Tam," sung by Mhairi Lawson (Concerto Caledonia) → Kirkby again, Arne's "Where the bee sucks" from The Tempest, 1746: Shakespeare, two minutes, and the same voice that opened the list singing a twelfth-century abbess.
