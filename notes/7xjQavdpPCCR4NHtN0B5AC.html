The world

Nashville 1960–64, seen from the publishing house. Willie Nelson arrives from Texas in 1960 having sold "Family Bible" for $50; Hank Cochran forgoes his own $50-a-week raise so Pamper Music — Hal Smith and Ray Price's company out in Goodlettsville — can hire him as a staff writer. In 1961 he is cutting solo demos for the song-pluggers, playing bass in Price's Cherokee Cowboys, and drinking at Tootsie's Orchid Lounge across the alley from the Ryman's back door with Cochran, Harlan Howard, Roger Miller, Mel Tillis and Bill Anderson. Within eighteen months his songs are hits for Billy Walker, Faron Young, Patsy Cline and Ray Price, and in August 1962 Joe Allison takes him into the Quonset Hut to cut …And Then I Wrote for Liberty. The sound is the Nashville Sound at its peak — Owen Bradley's strings and the Jordanaires on one side, the Cherokee Cowboys' 4/4 shuffle on the other — and, in between, a man alone with a guitar singing demos a beat behind the bar line.

Selection method

For each Willie composition: his own recording where the January sitting had it (the Texas Willie demos and the 1962 "Hello Walls"), otherwise the recording that made the song — Patsy's "Crazy", Billy Walker's "Funny How Time Slips Away", Faron Young's "Three Days", Ray Price's "Night Life" — plus the Liberty sides that were never anyone else's hit ("Mr. Record Man", "Touch Me", "Undo the Right", "Half a Man"). The ring is adjacency by publishing house and bar stool, not by sound: Howard's "Heartaches by the Number" (the song that made Pamper's writers' room), Cochran's "She's Got You" and "A Little Bitty Tear", Cochran singing Howard's "Sally Was a Good Old Girl" (a song about Tootsie's), Tillis/Dill's "Detroit City", Anderson's "Still", Miller's "Dang Me". Left out on purpose: Faron Young's "Hello Walls" (Willie's own is the anchor), "Family Bible" (Claude Gray's Feb 1960 Decca single isn't verifiably what streams), "Pretty Paper" (Christmas). Connective tissue & sequencing

  • Opens with "Mr. Record Man" — Willie's first Liberty single (recorded August 1961), a songwriter begging the DJ for a spin — then the first spin he actually got: Billy Walker's "Funny How Time Slips Away" (Columbia, June 1961, #23).
  • The demos as a spine: the Texas Willie publishing demos (Any Old Arms, Teardrops, Wink, Both Ends of the Candle, I've Seen All This World, Misery Mansion) recur every three or four tracks — short, solo, stark — so the polished hits around them keep being heard as what the pluggers did with the tape.
  • Patsy & Cochran: "Crazy" (Oct 1961, Bradley and the Jordanaires) → "She's Got You" (Cochran, early 1962) → Burl Ives's "A Little Bitty Tear" (Cochran again, cut at Bradley's studio on Feb 15 1961) — the Owen Bradley house style in three steps.
  • The Cherokee Cowboys axis: Ray Price twice — "Heartaches by the Number" (Howard, 1959, the shuffle Willie would be playing bass on by 1961) and, at the close, "Night Life" (1963): the 4/4 shuffle giving way to strings and countrypolitan, Willie's bar-room song sung by his boss. It is the longest track and the last word.
  • The Tootsie's regulars sit in the back half: Anderson's "Still" and Bare's "Detroit City" (both 1963) as the Nashville Sound at its most commercial, then Roger Miller's "Dang Me" (1964) as the circle's clown finally breaking out — the one place the energy jumps — before the last demo and the long slow close.
