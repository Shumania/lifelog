The world

Pulse minimalism as a lineage, 1964–2015. It begins on a Manhattan street corner: Louis Hardin — Moondog, the blind "Viking of Sixth Avenue" — drumming his own rounds and canons at 54th & 6th, where the young Philip Glass and Steve Reich found him (Glass housed him for a year in 1970; the three recorded together). From that corner the pulse moves downtown — Riley's In C generation, Reich's ECM-era ensembles, Glass's own ensemble, Meredith Monk's loft pieces — then west to John Adams and the California post-minimalists, and across the Atlantic to the Europeans who took the same idea toward film and the cathedral (Nyman, Mertens, ten Holt, Pärt). It lands in the 2000s–2010s "post-classical" room — Richter, Frahm — and finally back in Brooklyn with Dawn of Midi, three acoustic players reproducing Moondog's machine-steady pulse by hand.

Selection method

Adjacency by lineage and by hands, never Spotify-similar. Three real threads bind the picks: (1) people who knew each other — Moondog → Glass/Reich (recorded together 1970); Reich and Glass shared an ensemble in the late 60s; Monk, Reich and Glass were the downtown loft scene; Adams and Duckworth are the first post-minimalist generation; (2) one pianist's curation — Bruce Brubaker plays Riley, Monk, Duckworth and Adams here (his Codex, Piano Songs, Time Curve and Inner Cities albums are essentially a map of this music); (3) one label — ECM released both Music for 18 Musicians (1978) and Pärt's Tabula Rasa/Fratres (1984, ECM New Series No. 1), the record that carried holy minimalism to the same listeners. Nyman/Mertens/ten Holt are the European film-and-concert echo of the American pulse (Nyman literally coined the term "minimalism" for music in 1968). Connective tissue & sequencing

  • The street corner (1–2): two Moondog rounds from the 1969 Columbia LP — the Shazam anchor first, then Bird's Lament (for Charlie Parker), the tiny sax canon everyone has heard sampled.
  • Downtown pulse (3–6): Riley's Keyboard Study 2 (1965, the pre-In C loop idea) → Reich's Pulses (the ECM 1978 original — the sound of the pulse becoming an ensemble) → Glass's Opening → Monk's Ellis Island, where the pulse turns into a piano song.
  • Post-minimalist piano (7–8): Duckworth's Time Curve Preludes and Adams's China Gates, both Brubaker — same hands, same decade (1977–79), pulse softened into ripple.
  • Mallets and guitar (9–10): Reich twice more, later and brighter — Nagoya Marimbas and the Metheny Electric Counterpoint finale, the most rock-adjacent minute in the set.
  • The European echo (11–15): Nyman's Purcell-driven Chasing Sheep (a Baroque ground bass under a minimalist engine — the Bach listener will hear the bridge) and Time Lapse; Mertens's Struggle for Pleasure; ten Holt's four-piano Canto Ostinato; then the hush of Pärt's Fratres with Kremer and Jarrett.
  • Post-classical (16–18): Richter → Frahm's Says (a Reich pulse under a synth swell) → Glass's Metamorphosis Two, the quietest thing here.
  • Opens on the Shazam — Moondog's drum on the sidewalk. Closes on Dawn of Midi's Io: three humans holding a machine-steady groove in a Brooklyn room, 46 years later — the pulse comes home, and the dormant love gets re-heard.
