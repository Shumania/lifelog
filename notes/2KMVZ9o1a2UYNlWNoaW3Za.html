The world

The American folk revival between the Kingston Trio's "Tom Dooley" (a Capitol single, #1 on November 17, 1958) and the first Village singer-songwriter records of 1965–66: two rooms and three labels. The rooms are Chicago's Gate of Horn — the Albert Grossman club on North Dearborn where Odetta made her name (her 1957 Tradition LP is titled for those engagements, though it was cut in a studio) and where Elektra taped Bob Gibson and Bob Camp over three sets in April 1961 — and the Greenwich Village circuit of Gerde's, the Gaslight and Dave Van Ronk's apartment on Waverly Place, where a twenty-year-old Dylan lifted Van Ronk's "House of the Rising Sun" arrangement for his March 1962 debut. The labels are Vanguard (Doc Watson, Ian & Sylvia, Buffy Sainte-Marie, Mississippi John Hurt's post-rediscovery Today!, the Weavers' 1963 reunion), Elektra (Judy Collins, Phil Ochs, Fred Neil, Gibson & Camp) and Prestige (Van Ronk's Folksinger; Samuel Charters' 1964 Chicago sessions with a nineteen-year-old Terry Callier that Prestige sat on until January 1968). It is a repertoire world: railroad songs, gallows and gambling ballads, hammer songs and the movement hymn, each sung once, and then the new songs — Tyson's, Sainte-Marie's, Neil's, Hardin's, Callier's own — written inside those old modes.

Selection method

The door is Callier's record: five cuts, the Deluxe-edition IDs he actually sat with on Tuesday rather than the 2025 Remaster release — "900 Miles" (the Shazam, non-negotiable), the gambling song "Jack O'Diamonds", the dance tune "Cotton Eyed Joe", the Yeats setting "Golden Apples of the Sun" (Travis Edmonson's 1960 tune for "The Song of Wandering Aengus", the one Judy Collins named her 1962 album after), and his own "Spin, Spin, Spin". Connective tissue & sequencing

Sequenced by song-family, so each cluster is a conversation between readings of the same kind of song. (1) Railroad songs — "900 Miles" opens because it is the Shazam and the door; "500 Miles" answers it (the same lonesome-train lament rewritten by a woman from Cartersville, Georgia); "Tom Dooley" is the 1958 starting gun. (2) Money, gambling and the gallows — Axton's "Greenback Dollar" (his own song with Ken Ramsey, taped live at the Troubadour before the Kingston Trio made it a hit in 1963), Callier's "Jack O'Diamonds", Gibson & Camp's Atlanta murder ballad "Betty and Dupree", Van Ronk's "Cocaine Blues" (from Rev. Gary Davis), and Dylan's "House of the Risin' Sun" in Van Ronk's arrangement — the album Van Ronk didn't get to make first. (3) Hammer and work songs — Odetta's "Take This Hammer", Hurt's "Spike Driver Blues" (the John Henry song he first cut for Okeh in 1928, re-recorded for Vanguard in July 1964 after Tom Hoskins found him in Avalon, Mississippi), Watson's "Deep River Blues" (the Delmore Brothers' tune on his 1964 Vanguard debut), and Callier's "Cotton Eyed Joe" to lift the floor. (4) The movement — Seeger's "We Shall Overcome" and Ochs' "Power and Glory", the two public songs, placed at the center as the revival's peak. (5) Love lyrics — Collins' "Wild Mountain Thyme" (Francis McPeake's Belfast adaptation, on her 1961 Elektra debut), Callier's "Golden Apples", Ian Tyson's "Four Strong Winds" (Vanguard, September 1963). (6) The new songs in the old modes — Sainte-Marie's "Cod'ine" (It's My Way!, January 1964), Neil's "Other Side to This Life" (Bleecker & MacDougal, 1965), Hardin's "Reason to Believe" (Tim Hardin 1, July 1966) — the moment the songbook stopped being traditional. It closes with the Weavers singing "Goodnight Irene" at their May 2, 1963 Carnegie Hall reunion — the older generation saying goodnight — and then Callier's own "Spin, Spin, Spin": the record opened with him singing the tradition and ends with him writing inside it, which is exactly where he went next (Cadet, Charles Stepney, What Color Is Love). 86 minutes, mid register: the hushed Callier cuts are spaced by the Trio, Axton, Odetta, Watson and the Weavers so the list never settles into a whisper.
