Seed list supplied with the prompt (Dixon → Whole Lotta Love, Killing Floor → Lemon Song, Jake Holmes, Anne Bredon, etc.), each mapping verified against documented cover/credit histories before inclusion; the Bobby Parker → Moby Dick riff lineage was corroborated by press/obituary sources, and one seed item (Chuck Berry's "You Can't Catch Me" as a Stones source) failed verification and was dropped.

The world

This is the record collection two sets of British teenagers ransacked: the Chess and Sun 45s and prewar 78s that crossed the Atlantic in merchant-sailor duffel bags and mail-order boxes. The playlist walks the migration itself — Blind Willie Johnson's gospel slide and the Delta 78s of 1927–39, up Highway 61 into the electrified Chicago of Chess Records (Muddy, Wolf, Dixon's pen behind both), sideways into Excello swamp blues and Stax/Atlantic soul, and out through the rock'n'roll singles of Chuck Berry and Buddy Holly — ending where the borrowing got litigious: Joan Baez's Anne Bredon arrangement and Jake Holmes' "Dazed and Confused," the two non-roots American origins Zeppelin absorbed whole.

The paper trail: Willie Dixon wrote five of these (11, 12, 15, 16, 17, 18 — six sides counting co-writes) and fought for them twice — Arc Music sued over "Bring It On Home" (settled 1972), and Dixon himself sued over "Whole Lotta Love" (settled 1985; he used the money to found the Blues Heaven Foundation). Chester Burnett (Howlin' Wolf) got a settlement and credit when "Killing Floor" became "The Lemon Song." Jake Holmes — who opened for the Yardbirds in 1967 and watched his song walk off with them — sued in 2010; "Dazed and Confused" is now credited "inspired by Jake Holmes." Anne Bredon didn't learn Zeppelin had recorded her song (via Baez's In Concert arrangement, which Page learned from the record) until the 1980s; she's been credited and paid since 1990. Ritchie Valens' publisher extracted a settlement over "Boogie with Stu" — ironically a track Zeppelin says they'd credited "Mrs. Valens" on precisely to route money to Ritchie's mother. The Stones were mostly cleaner — they cut covers with credits, sent "Little Red Rooster" to UK #1 as a Dixon blues, and Wilkins' "Prodigal Son" got its author credit corrected after early Beggars Banquet pressings said "Jagger/Richards."

Selection method

Mapping-first, not vibe-first: a track qualified only if the Zeppelin/Stones derivation is documented (writer credits, settlements, or well-attested session history) — one seed suggestion (Chuck Berry "You Can't Catch Me" → Stones) failed verification and was dropped; Bobby Parker → "Moby Dick" was verified via multiple press sources before inclusion. Original-era recordings preferred throughout: Bukka White is the 1937 Vocalion side (from the Parchman Farm compilation), NOT the 1963 Sonet re-record; Howlin' Wolf's "Little Red Rooster" is the 1961 Chess original, not the 1971 London Sessions remake (whose band, in a nice bit of circularity, included Watts and Wyman); Muddy's "I Just Want to Make Love to You" is the 1954 single version; Bobby Parker is the original V-Tone 45 master. Substitutions of note: Robert Wilkins appears via the 1929 Victor side "That's No Way to Get Along" because the Spotify version of his 1964 gospel rework "Prodigal Son" (the arrangement the Stones actually followed) is a 10-minute take that would sink the sequence; Fred McDowell's "You Got to Move" is his 1960s Arhoolie-era recording via the Steakbone Slide Guitar compilation; Alvin Robinson's side carries the compilation title "I'm Just a Down Home Girl"; Robert Johnson's "Love in Vain"/"Stop Breakin' Down" are the Take 1 masters as compiled on King of the Delta Blues.

Connective tissue & sequencing

Chronological, so the playlist tells the migration story itself:

  • Delta & gospel 78s (1–9, 1927–39): opens with Blind Willie Johnson's two gospel-blues sides — the deepest stratum Zeppelin ever mined — through Memphis Minnie, Wilkins, three Robert Johnsons (the only artist here both bands drew from), Bukka White, and Lead Belly's gallows ballad.
  • Electrification (10–22, 1951–67): Highway 61 to Chicago. Wolf's "How Many More Years" (recorded at Sun, released on Chess) opens the electric era; the Dixon songbook dominates the middle — the single biggest authorial presence on the playlist — flanked by Excello's swamp (Slim Harpo twice) and closing on Albert King's Stax blues. McDowell's hill-country "You Got to Move" sits here as the tradition persisting inside the electric years.
  • Rock'n'roll 45s (23–30, 1957–61): the Stones' apprenticeship — three Chuck Berrys including "Come On," the literal first Stones single — plus Holly's Bo Diddley-beat "Not Fade Away" next to Diddley's own "Mona" (same beat, one track apart), Valens' "Ooh! My Head" and Bobby Parker's "Watch Your Step" carrying the Zeppelin thread through the section.
  • Soul (31–38, 1961–64): what the Stones were covering in real time as new releases, not crate-digging — Muscle Shoals (Alexander), Motown (Gaye), Stax (Rufus Thomas), New Orleans (Irma Thomas, Robinson), and the Atlantic/SAR axis (Covay, Burke, the Valentinos — "It's All Over Now" became their first UK #1 within months of Womack's original).
  • Coda (39–40, 1962/1967): the two American originals that aren't roots music at all — Baez's stark folk arrangement and Jake Holmes' acid-folk bassline — i.e., the borrowing habit carried right up to the eve of Led Zeppelin I. Opens on a 1927 gospel slide guitar; closes on the 1967 song that became the centerpiece of Zeppelin's debut two years later — the whole twelve-bar transatlantic heist in forty tracks.
