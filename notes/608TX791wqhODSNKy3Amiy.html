The world

Mali's songbook — the pieces every Bamako band ends up playing, the way every New York band ends up playing "Take Five" (Afropop's own line about "Jarabi"). The repertoire has three sources: the jeli (griot) standards of the Manden heartland around Kita and Kangaba — "Lamban" (the griots' own song, a melody said to be 800 years old), "Kaira" (Sidiki Diabaté's kora piece, the one that made his son's name in 1988), "Jarabi" (passion), the "Touramagan" epic — carried on kora, ngoni and the great praise voices; the modern songs that became standards the moment they were cut — Salif Keita's "Mandjou" (Les Ambassadeurs, Abidjan 1978), Oumou Sangaré's Wassoulou "Diaraby Nene" (1989), Boubacar Traoré's "Mariama Kaba", Habib Koité's "Cigarette A Bana", Amadou & Mariam's "Je Pense A Toi"; and the Niger-bend and desert songs with named authors — Ali Farka Touré's "Ai Du", Ibrahim Ag Alhabib's "Cler Achel", Ousmane Ag Mossa's "Toumast anlet". Bambara, Maninka, Wassoulou, Songhai, Peul, Tamasheq and a little French — none of it in English.

Selection method

Connective tissue & sequencing

The running order walks south to north and, inside each stop, roughly oldest to newest — Kita to Kidal, 114 minutes.

  • The griot room (1–5): two koras opening with "Bi Lamban" (Toumani and Ballaké playing their fathers' 1970 duet forward), Toumani alone on "Kaira", then the voices arrive — Kassé Mady on "Jarabi", the four-ngara summit on "Touramagan" — and the whole thing goes electric in Abidjan with "Mandjou": Kanté Manfila's guitar and Salif's voice turning a praise song into a twelve-minute big-band side.
  • Wassoulou and the Bamako songwriters (6–12): Oumou's "Diaraby Nene" is the hinge (a teenager's love song in a griot man's country); then the guitar-and-voice writers — Kar Kar's "Mariama Kaba" (for his late wife), Habib Koité, Amadou & Mariam's tabla-led ballad — before Bassekou's ngoni band ("Jama ko", recorded during the March 2012 coup as a call for unity) hands to Rokia's Gretsch and Fatoumata's migrant love song "Nterini".
  • The river (13–17): Ali Farka's "Ai Du" with Ry Cooder, his last session with Toumani ("Kala Djula", London 2005, released 2010), his Niafunké pupil Afel Bocoum, his son with Khruangbin ("Tongo Barra" — Andrew's own way in), and Songhoy Blues' "Soubour" ("patience", written for the northern refugees in Bamako) as the one true electric peak.
  • The desert (18–19): "Cler Achel" and Tamikrest's "Toumast anlet" — Kidal's two generations of the same guitar school.
  • Closes on Salif Keita's "Folon" (1995) — "the past" — the voice from track 5 seventeen years older, singing about the country the whole playlist just crossed.
