The world

Vienna, 1815–1826: Beethoven after the deafness is total, writing for an audience he can only imagine. The late voice is one voice whichever instrument carries it — the piano of Opp. 101–111 and the Diabelli, the cello of Op. 102, the quartet of Opp. 127–135 — and its fingerprints are constant: the fugue as confession rather than display, the slow movement as hymn (Heiliger Dankgesang, Cavatina, Arietta), variation as a way of dissolving a tune into light, and the inward German markings that replace the Italian tempo words — mit der innigsten Empfindung, Gesangvoll, Neue Kraft fühlend, cantante tranquillo. "Gesangvoll" — song-full — is Beethoven's own word over the Op. 109 finale Uchida played twice.

Selection method

A voice study, not a scene: one work per opus, one recording per work, every late instrumental opus of consequence from Op. 101 to Op. 135 represented by the movement where the late voice is most audible (the adagios, the hymns, the one fugue that tears). Performers chosen for the recording's standing, one per work, so the voice is heard through fourteen different hands: Levit's 2013 Sony debut (Op. 101); Isserlis & Levin on fortepiano, Hyperion 2014 (Op. 102/2); Pollini's 1976 DG Hammerklavier Adagio; Uchida live at Suntory Hall, Oct 2025 (Opp. 109/110/111 — the anchors, kept to three); Kovacevich's 1968 Philips Diabelli; Schiff in 1977, aged 23, on Beethoven's own Broadwood in the Hungarian National Museum (Op. 126/3); Takács 2004 (Op. 127); Quartetto Italiano, Vevey 1967–69 (Op. 132); the Busch Quartet in New York, June 1941 (Op. 130 Cavatina); Quatuor Ébène live in Tokyo, 2020 (Op. 133); Quatuor Diotima 2026 (Op. 131); Alban Berg Quartett live at the Konzerthaus, 1989 (Op. 135). Missa solemnis and the Ninth are deliberately absent — instrumental only. Connective tissue & sequencing

  • Chronological, 1815 → 1826, piano half then quartet half, with the cello sonata (1815) placed second — the earliest of the late works, its Adagio the first of these hymns.
  • Opens on Op. 101's mit der innigsten Empfindung — four minutes that announce the whole manner — and the same marking returns over the Op. 109 finale (Gesangvoll, mit innigster Empfindung), the playlist's name and its first anchor.
  • The Uchida core (4–6) runs the three last sonatas in order and ends on the Arietta's twenty minutes — the movement Andrew went back to alone on Jul 28.
  • The pivot (7–8): Diabelli's closing minuet and the little E-flat Bagatelle — the voice at its most modest, before the quartets.
  • Quartets: Op. 127's variation-adagio → the Heiliger Dankgesang (Op. 132, nineteen minutes of convalescence) → the Cavatina (Op. 130, Busch 1941 — the recording that defined it) → the Große Fuge (the Cavatina's own original finale, the one storm here) → Op. 131's opening fugue, the fugue answered in grief → closes on Op. 135's Lento assai, cantante tranquillo, the slow movement of the last work he completed, October 1826.
  • Runtime ≈ 2 h 29 min; the long movements are quoted whole because the voice lives in their spans.
