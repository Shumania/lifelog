The world

The prelude is the piano's shortest form and its oldest habit. In 1722 Bach wrote one in every key to open each fugue of the Well-Tempered Clavier; in a damp monastery cell at Valldemossa, Chopin — who had carried the WTC to Majorca and played it daily — finished a set of 24 with no fugues attached: one per key, ordered around the circle of fifths, each a complete thought in a minute or three, published 1839. After that, "24 preludes" became a rite of passage. Scriabin (Op. 11, 1888–96), Shostakovich (Op. 87, 1950–51, written after he judged the Leipzig Bach bicentennial and heard Tatiana Nikolayeva play the 48) and Kapustin (Op. 53, 1988) kept Chopin's exact key order, so "No. 4" means E minor in every generation; Rachmaninoff assembled his 24 across three opus numbers; the French — Alkan, Debussy, Ravel, Messiaen, Dutilleux — kept the name and let the key go; Gershwin premiered three in a Manhattan hotel in 1926; Chilly Gonzales wrote one in 5/4 built on Bach's C-major arpeggio, and Ott put it between Chopin's No. 15 and No. 16. This playlist plays that lineage as a single hour.

Connective tissue & sequencing

Sequenced by key, not by date. Because the cycles after Chopin keep his numbering, a shared number is a shared key across a century — so the hour is five key-clusters, each opened by Chopin, then a French descent.

  • C major (1–3): Bach's arpeggios → Chopin's No. 1, the same figure made agitated → Shostakovich's Op. 87 No. 1, a sarabande-quiet Moderato and the most explicit WTC homage of the twentieth century. Three C majors, 1722 / 1839 / 1951.
  • E minor (4–6): Chopin's Largo (played, with No. 6, at his funeral) → Scriabin's Op. 11 No. 4, Lento — the young Scriabin who, the story goes, slept with Chopin's scores under his pillow → Shostakovich's Op. 87 No. 4, Andante. Same slot, same key, three answers.
  • B minor (7–10): Chopin's No. 6, the left-hand cello melody the Spotify mix served him twice in October → Lyadov's Op. 11 No. 1 (St Petersburg, 1886 — the miniaturist's one famous page) → Szymanowski's Op. 1 No. 1 (1900, a Polish eighteen-year-old's first opus, Chopin-haunted) → Rachmaninoff's Op. 32 No. 10 (1910), his own favourite of all his preludes, prompted by Böcklin's painting Die Heimkehr — the summit of the hour, placed at the midpoint so everything after is descent.
  • The black keys (11–14): the Raindrop in D-flat (George Sand's story of the rain on the Valldemossa roof) → Gonzales's C-sharp major, Bach's opening arpeggio recast in 5/4 — Ott titled it No Roadmap to Adulthood in her sequence → Gershwin's "sort of blues lullaby" in C-sharp minor, Levant 1944 → Kapustin's No. 15, a jazz prelude sitting in Chopin's D-flat slot.
  • B-flat (15–16): Chopin's Cantabile → Asal's Scriabin No. 21: the seed's label and generation answering the seed.
  • France, into silence (17–21): Alkan — Chopin's friend and Square d'Orléans neighbour — and his 1847 Song of the madwoman by the sea → Debussy's footsteps in the snow (1910) → Ravel's one-page Prélude (1913, written as a Conservatoire sight-reading test) → Messiaen's dove (1929, his first published piano music) → Dutilleux's D'ombre et de silence (1973).
  • Opens on the piece every cycle here is answering — Bach's C major. Closes on a title: Of shadow and silence.
