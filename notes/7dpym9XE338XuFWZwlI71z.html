Seeds

Andrew's ask on the morning of Sep 21, in the same breath as the new Beta Band Discovery card (#272): "build a playlist of unlistened-to artists who inspired the Beta Band." Two constraints, both hard: the artists had to be documented influences (not guesses, not Spotify-similar), and Andrew had to have zero exposure to them — no plays, no likes, under any spelling.

The evidence base was built the same morning from primary sources: Steve Mason's 2016 Baker's Dozen for The Quietus (thirteen favourite records, several tied explicitly to the Beta Band years), John Maclean's 2018 Stereogum interview for the Three E.P.'s anniversary, Mason's 2001 Pitchfork interview, and the WhoSampled credit list for every cleared sample on the four Beta Band albums. Forty-three artists came out of that pass. Twenty-six were already in the history and were set aside: Nas (111 plays), Beastie Boys, Brian Eno, Morricone, De La Soul, the Beach Boys, Kraftwerk, Happy Mondays, the Pharcyde, Horace Andy and Augustus Pablo (the whole Santic comp Mason bought "when we first started writing as the Beta Band" is already known here), Grandmaster Flash, the Stone Roses, Harry Nilsson, Steve Miller Band, Les Baxter and more. Two further casualties fell to the second look: Rammellzee & K-Rob's Beat Bop passed the track-ID check but had been played twice under another release, and Lead Belly hid behind a space in his name (The Gallis Pole, one play). Both were pulled. What remained is this: the unheard half of a record collection.

The title comes from Mason's own account of the band's beginnings — the squat in Shepherd's Bush where the first demos were recorded in 1995 and where certain records were played "many a night."

The world

This is not one scene; it is one band's ears. The Beta Band's trick was to hear Fife folk, hip-hop sampling, dub bass and krautrock patience as a single thing, and the records here are the raw material for that trick, arranged as the four strands the band members themselves describe.

The crate is the hip-hop half of their method made literal: the actual records they lifted from. Curtis Knight — Jimi Hendrix's old bandleader, recording without him in 1970 — sits underneath Dry the Rain. Bob James's Take Me to the Mardi Gras, the CTI-era bell-and-break that half of golden-age hip-hop is built on, is the floor of Dance O'er the Border; Nautilus is its dark twin from the album before, the most-sampled Bob James of all and the right way to open a record about crate-digging. Ohio Express, the Kasenetz-Katz bubblegum factory, gave up Jacksonville Station for The Beta Band Rap. John Barry's main title for Disney's The Black Hole — a lush, ominous 1979 space theme — is the ghost inside It's Not Too Beautiful.

The gateway is Steve Mason's teenage listening, told in his own order. Dead Kennedys at age ten ("where the Sex Pistols failed, Dead Kennedys succeeded — they were really intelligent"). Then PiL's Metal Box: "This was before I got into dub and I'd never heard a record with that much bass on it before." Then the dub itself, arrived at through John Lydon: Mason read Lydon praising Tapper Zukie's M.P.L.A. and "went straight out and got that." Siouxsie and the Banshees is the one strand that runs to the end of the band's life — Painted Bird was cleared as the sample under Liquid Bird on Heroes to Zeros, the last album. The Prisoners' The Last Fourfathers is Mason's Medway mod-garage pick, the Hammond-and-fuzz sound that "sowed the seeds for bands like Inspiral Carpets and The Charlatans."

The vocabulary is hip-hop as the band actually used it — sample craft and intelligence rather than swagger. Paris's Sleeping With the Enemy is Mason's pick for content "at least Dead Kennedys level of intelligence, if not more … the album that Chuck D always wanted to make"; The Days of Old is its reflective centre. KRS-One is on Maclean's short list alongside Wu-Tang, De La Soul and the Beastie Boys. Company Flow is what Mason told Pitchfork he was listening to in 2001, in the middle of making Hot Shots II: "all I listen to currently is hip-hop — Company Flow, the Beatnuts."

The comedown is the KLF's Chill Out, the record both Mason and Maclean name independently as the template for how a Beta Band record should flow — "any record that flowed or had skits in between" (Mason); "used the samples amazingly and had never been bettered" (Maclean). Chill Out itself is off Spotify; Come Down Dawn is the KLF's own 2021 re-edit of it with the uncleared Elvis and Fleetwood Mac lifted out, still the same steel-guitar-and-sheep drift across the American South.

Selection method

Adjacency was documentary, never algorithmic: every artist is either named by a Beta Band member as an influence or favourite (Quietus Baker's Dozen, Stereogum, Pitchfork) or is a WhoSampled-credited sample source on a Beta Band track. Critic-drawn comparisons (Can, Neu!, the Beach Boys' Friends-era) were not used unless a member also said it — Kraftwerk was the only krautrock name confirmed in the band's own words, and Kraftwerk is heard.

Exposure was checked three ways before anything was built: consumption_history by artist, listening_history by artist, and Liked Songs joined to the tracks table — then repeated by track title and by name variant (Leadbelly/Lead Belly, PiL/Public Image Ltd., KRS/Boogie Down, KLF/Justified Ancients). Heard-vs-new verified by bare track ID pre-build: 16 of 16 new; anchors = none — Andrew's history contains no play or like by any of these twelve artists.

Where the literally-sampled recording exists on Spotify it was used (Hi-Low, Take Me to the Mardi Gras, Jacksonville Station, Painted Bird, and Barry's actual Black Hole main title rather than a stand-in). Where a record was unavailable it was left out rather than faked: Bad Bascomb's Black Grass (the other Dry the Rain sample) isn't on Spotify; All the People's Cramp Your Style likewise. Bonnie Tyler's Total Eclipse of the Heart is a genuine cleared sample (The Hard One) and Andrew has never played it, but it is a found object rather than an influence, and it was the one documented source deliberately skipped for the sake of the listen.

Connective tissue & sequencing

Four strands, in the order the band itself would tell it: what they sampled → what Mason grew up on → the hip-hop they spoke → the record that taught them how a whole album should breathe.

The opener is a groove rather than a statement: Nautilus, dark and patient, so that Hi-Low — the sound under Dry the Rain — lands second, where the anchor always sits. The crate runs roughly chronologically from there (1969–1979) and ends on Barry's ominous space theme, whose spookiness hands straight into the surf-noir guitar intro of Holiday in Cambodia. The gateway then follows Mason's own biography: punk → PiL → the Lydon-recommended dub, so Poptones (Jah Wobble's bass) sets up two Tapper Zukie sides rather than the other way round. Painted Bird and Mrs Fothergill close the strand as the two "guitar band" records — one sampled, one loved.

The hip-hop strand is chronological (1992 → 1993 → 1997) and deliberately opens on the mellowest of the three, The Days of Old, so the temperature rises through Sound of da Police into Company Flow's dust-and-static, the harshest point of the playlist. The KLF closes for two reasons: it is the record about flow that the band cite as their model, and Come Down Dawn is a comedown by name — fifteen minutes of steel guitar and radio static to drift out on after the noise, exactly where the Beta Band would have put it.
