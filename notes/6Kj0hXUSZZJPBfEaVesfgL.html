The world

Around 2016 a handful of small analog labels — Daptone in Brooklyn (Sharon Jones, Charles Bradley, later Jalen Ngonda and its sweet-soul imprint Penrose in San Diego), Big Crown in New York (Lady Wray, Holy Hive, Brainstory, Bobby Oroza's Timmion sides), Colemine in Ohio (Durand Jones, Monophonics, Say She She) — turned a record-collector obsession into a working scene. The sound is late-'60s/early-'70s soul recorded the way it was originally made: live to tape, one room, falsetto leads, drums played soft. On the West Coast it fused with the lowrider oldies tradition — the Brenton Wood and Delfonics 45s that Chicano car clubs have kept alive since the '70s — and produced Thee Sacred Souls, Thee Sinseers, The Altons and Los Yesterdays, whose slow-dances are as much about East LA as Memphis.

Running alongside it, a generation older, is neo-soul — the 1995–2000 lineage of Maxwell, D'Angelo, Badu and Meshell Ndegeocello, who first went back to Marvin, Leon Ware and Curtis for the same reasons and are all still releasing (Badu with The Alchemist and Meshell's covers record both landed in 2026). And in London the two currents meet: Cleo Sol and SAULT's Inflo productions, Michael Kiwanuka's Danger Mouse–produced KIWANUKA, Lianne La Havas, Jungle's disco-soul, and the Blood Orange / Sudan Archives art-R&B fringe. One thread: soul made after 2016 by people who learned it from records rather than radio.

Selection method

Adjacency by label and scene, never by algorithm. Starting from Penrose (Thee Sacred Souls) I took the label's own roster (Thee Sinseers, The Altons, Los Yesterdays), then its parent Daptone (Jalen Ngonda), then the two sister labels that share bands and producers with it — Big Crown (Holy Hive, Brainstory, Lady Wray, Bobby Oroza) and Colemine (Durand Jones, Aaron Frazer's solo record cut with Dan Auerbach, Say She She, Monophonics with Kelly Finnigan). Adrian Quesada connects Black Pumas to the bolero side of the same revival. The neo-soul half takes one track per card artist, preferring 2026 releases the cards surfaced (Badu/Alchemist, Meshell/La Havas, Jungle's Sunshine) so the set reads as a live scene rather than a retrospective.

Connective tissue & sequencing

Four movements, each a label-family, moving from the youngest scene to its elders:

  1. Penrose / lowrider (1–6): Can I Call You Rose? opens because it is the thesis — falsetto, brushed snare, a two-chord slow-dance. Thee Sinseers and The Altons are literally the same San Diego / LA circle (Joey Quiñones has played in both); Los Yesterdays add the Chicano-oldies accent; Bobby Oroza (Helsinki, but recorded by Cold Diamond & Mink for the same collectors) shows how far the 45 culture travels; Jalen Ngonda closes the movement with the purest Daptone voice of the decade.
  2. Big Crown / Colemine (7–13): the Midwest and New York wing. Is It Any Wonder? is Aaron Frazer's falsetto inside Durand Jones's band, then his own Bad News — one voice across two records. Holy Hive and Brainstory keep the tempo down; Lady Wray is the scene's living link to '90s R&B; Say She She and Monophonics lift it slightly toward disco-soul and psych.
  3. Texas / Latin soul (14–15): Black Pumas' Ice Cream (Pay Phone) is the revival's arena-scale version; Adrian Quesada's bolero with iLe pivots the whole set — Spanish-language, the one track that shows the sweet-soul revival is also a Latin-American story.
  4. Neo-soul elders and London (16–25): Maxwell's Pretty Wings is the ancestor these bands all cite; Badu with The Alchemist and Meshell with Lianne La Havas are the elders' 2026 answers; La Havas → Cleo Sol → SAULT is one Inflo-adjacent London arc; Jungle and Sudan Archives raise the pulse for a late-set dance; Blood Orange's Vivid Light cools it; Kiwanuka's seven-minute Light closes as a hymn — the same warmth as the opener, an hour and a half later.
