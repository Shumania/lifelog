The world

The electric bass became a lead voice inside Black American popular music in about a decade — Jamerson's Motown counter-melodies, then Larry Graham's thumb (1969), then a generation who treated the Fender as a singer: Rocco Prestia's fingerstyle sixteenths in Oakland, Louis Johnson's slap in L.A., Bootsy's envelope-filtered "space bass" in the P-Funk mothership, Jaco's fretless on his 1976 debut, Bernard Edwards' eight bars that hip-hop was built on. This playlist is that voice traced forward through its own lineage — the funk/R&B line, not the rock or jazz one — one recording per player and one per song, from Jamerson lying on the Hitsville floor in 1971 to the LA/London scene where Thundercat, Joe Dart, Blu DeTiger and Rocco Palladino (Pino's son) now carry it. Every track here is chosen because the bass IS the hook, the melody or the argument; the singers are passengers.

Selection method

Voice study, one recording per bassist and one per composition — nobody appears twice as a player (so Mac Miller "What's the Use?" and Flying Lotus's Flamagra, both Thundercat bass, were left out; Thundercat gets exactly one slot). Every bass credit was verified against transcriptions, session credits and interviews before inclusion — Spotify's artist field never names the bassist: Jamerson on "What's Going On" (the drunk-on-the-floor session, 1971), Chuck Rainey with Bernard Purdie on "Rock Steady", Francis "Rocco" Prestia on "Soul Vaccination", Nathan Watts' eight-note line on "I Wish" (reportedly three years into playing electric bass), Louis Johnson on "Strawberry Letter 23", Bernard Edwards on "Good Times" (recreated by Chip Shearin for "Rapper's Delight"), Mark Adams on "Just a Touch of Love", Marcus Miller on Luther's "Never Too Much", Mark King on "Something About You", Stuart Zender on "Virtual Insanity", Raphael Saadiq (bass and vocals, per the album credits) on "Dance Tonight", Joe Dart on "Dean Town", Rocco Palladino across Black Classical Music. Jamiroquai's "Space Cowboy" was dropped on exactly this test — Zender wrote the line but has said Paul Powell played the album take — so the Zender slot went to "Virtual Insanity". Connective tissue & sequencing

Chronological, 1971 → 2025, with one deliberate bend at the end. It opens on Jamerson because half the players on this list have named him as the source — Prestia, Jaco and Marcus Miller on record — and because "What's Going On" is the record where a Motown bass line became the lead singer's equal. Rainey and Prestia (1971–73) are the two fingerstyle schools — New York session and Oakland horn band — before the thumb takes over: Watts, then Jaco and Clarke back to back as 1976's two statements of the bass as a solo voice (Jaco's debut with Sam & Dave singing; Clarke's "School Days" the first bass-hero anthem), then Bootsy and Louis Johnson as 1977's two faces of slap — one psychedelic and sung, one radio-bright. Edwards into Mark Adams is 1979 disco-funk, the two eight-bar vamps that defined the decade's dance floors, and Marcus Miller's "Never Too Much" (1981) is the same New York studio world turning into the R&B of the eighties. Level 42 and Jamiroquai are the British answer — Mark King's slap pop (1985) and Zender's Brit-funk (1996) — bracketing Victor Wooten (1996), who turns Graham's thumb into pure virtuoso solo bass. Lucy Pearl's Saadiq (2000) is the bridge from Tony! Toni! Toné! to the neo-soul room Thundercat grew up in. The last stretch is the now: Esperanza's power-trio "Good Lava" (2016), Vulfpeck's "Dean Town" (2016 — Joe Dart's open tribute to Jaco's "Teen Town", closing the loop to track 5), Blu DeTiger (2020, the bassist as frontwoman), then the hush of Pino & Blake Mills (2025) — the one quiet track, placed late so the funk spine carries the energy stamp honestly — into Yussef Dayes with Rocco Palladino (2023), father to son in consecutive tracks. It closes on "Black Qualls" out of strict order because it is the door: Thundercat's bass carrying Steve Lacy, Childish Gambino and Steve Arrington — the Slave singer whose band is track 10 — so the lineage literally sings on the last track, and because that Jul 15 Vashon sitting is where this whole playlist came from.
