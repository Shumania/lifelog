The world

New York, 1988–1996: the seven years when hip-hop's producers stopped digging James Brown and started digging Blue Note, Prestige, CTI and Flying Dutchman — and the jazz musicians noticed. Stetsasonic's manifesto (1988) names the argument; Gang Starr's "Jazz Thing" (written for Spike Lee's Mo' Better Blues, 1990) turns it into a history lesson; Tribe puts Ron Carter in the studio on The Low End Theory; Guru flies Donald Byrd, Roy Ayers and Branford Marsalis in for Jazzmatazz; and by 1993 Digable Planets and Us3 have the sound on the pop charts. It radiated outward — Toronto (Dream Warriors), Oakland (Souls of Mischief), LA (Pharcyde), Chicago (Common), Philadelphia (The Roots playing it live with an actual band) — but the center of gravity is Queens, Brooklyn and the Native Tongues/Gang Starr Foundation circle. The sound: upright-bass loops, Rhodes and vibes, horn stabs, mid-tempo boom-bap, and MCs who rap like they're sitting in.

Connective tissue & sequencing

  • The argument (1–3): Stetsasonic's "Talkin' All That Jazz" opens because it's the thesis statement; it's built on Lonnie Liston Smith's Expansions, which follows so you hear the loop unspooled; Gang Starr's "Jazz Thing" closes the trio as the scholarly version of the same claim (Branford Marsalis co-wrote it).
  • Queens and the Native Tongues (4–8): Can I Kick It? → Main Source's "Looking at the Front Door" → its source, Donald Byrd's "Think Twice" (1975, the Stepping Into Tomorrow Mizell Brothers era) → De La Soul's "Stakes Is High" (Dilla flipping Ahmad Jamal) → Tribe's "Verses from the Abstract" with Ron Carter's live upright — the moment the sampled bassist walks into the room.
  • The crossover year, 1993 (9–11): two Digable Planets cuts from Reachin' (the hit, then the deeper "Nickel Bags") into Us3's "Cantaloop" — Blue Note's own sanctioned flip of Herbie Hancock, the label finally cashing in on its catalogue.
  • Jazzmatazz (12–13): Guru with Donald Byrd live on "Loungin'" — Byrd is the hinge of the whole playlist, sampled in track 6 and playing in track 12 — then "For You" with Meshell Ndegeocello and Kenny Garrett, the Vol. II track that ties tonight to Andrew's August.
  • Boom-bap's jazz school (14–16): Dream Warriors' Quincy Jones flip from Toronto, then the Pete Rock pair — "Straighten It Out" into "T.R.O.Y.", the two horn-loop masterpieces from Mecca and the Soul Brother.
  • The echoes (17–20): Oakland (Souls of Mischief over Billy Cobham), Chicago (Common over Ahmad Jamal), LA (Pharcyde's Runnin', Dilla over Stan Getz/Luiz Bonfá), Philadelphia (The Roots' "Proceed" — no samples, a band doing it live, which is where the scene was heading).
  • Closes on Roy Ayers' "Everybody Loves the Sunshine" (1976) — one of the most-sampled records in hip-hop and the sunset every one of these producers was reaching for; ending on the source rather than a flip leaves the loop open.
