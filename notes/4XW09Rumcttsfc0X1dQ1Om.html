The world

San Francisco, 1967–69: Sly Stone's Family Stone was the first integrated, mixed-gender band in American pop — Black and white, men and women, a horn section, fuzz guitar, gospel voices trading lines, and Larry Graham's thumb inventing slap bass. Then the 1971 retreat into the home studio with a drum machine (Riot) and the lean clavinet funk of Fresh (1973). Every strand of 1970s Black pop pulled on one of those threads: Motown's psychedelic soul, Miles's and Herbie's electric turn, George Clinton's rock-funk, the horn bands of Oakland and Dayton, and finally Prince's Minneapolis. This is that family tree, read in roughly the order it grew, 1968 → 1980.

The Isleys are the one node that is a sibling rather than a child — "It's Your Thing" was their answer to Berry Gordy, cut on their own T-Neck label in early 1969 — and it stands here as the same season's declaration of self-determination, with Andrew's play as the reason.

Connective tissue & sequencing

  • The root (1–2): "Sing a Simple Song" (1968) — the Family Stone engine, every voice taking a line; "Thank You (Falettinme Be Mice Elf Agin)" (Dec 1969) — the record on which Larry Graham's thumb-slap became a lead instrument.
  • The first answers (3–5): Otis Williams played "Dance to the Music" for Norman Whitfield and asked for something like it — "Cloud Nine" (Oct 1968) was Motown's psychedelic-soul reply within months. The Isleys' T-Neck declaration (1969). Curtis Mayfield's "(Don't Worry) If There Is a Hell Below" (1970) — Stand!-era protest funk, his first record after the Impressions.
  • The underground (6–9): Clinton rebuilt Funkadelic as a Sly-style rock-funk band — "Hit It and Quit It" (1971) with Bernie Worrell's organ; Shuggie Otis's "Ice Cold Daydream" (1971), the Los Angeles teenager with Sly's fuzz; Stevie's "Maybe Your Baby" (1972) travels the same slowed-down terrain as Riot; Miles's "Black Satin" (1972) — Miles named Sly and James Brown as the models for On the Corner.
  • 1973, the Family Stone diaspora (10–12): "In Time" (Fresh) — the story goes that Miles made his band listen to it on repeat; Betty Davis's debut was produced by Family Stone drummer Greg Errico with Larry Graham on bass and the Pointer Sisters singing behind her; Herbie's "Sly" is literally named for him ("'Sly' was for Sly Stone").
  • The horn bands, Bay Area and Midwest (13–19): Oakland's Tower of Power ("What Is Hip?", 1973) → Kool & the Gang's Jersey City jazz-funk → Mandrill's Brooklyn Afro-Latin funk ("Fencewalk", Composite Truth, 1973) → Graham Central Station's debut ("Hair", 1973/74 — Larry Graham's own band after leaving Sly in 1972) → Dayton's Ohio Players → Chicago's Rufus with Chaka Khan singing a song Stevie wrote for them → the Pointer Sisters back on their own record ("How Long", Steppin', 1975), the same Oakland sisters who sang on Betty Davis.
  • Opens with the simplest statement of the sound; closes on "Uptown" (Dirty Mind, 1980) — Prince restating the Family Stone's integrated dream for Minneapolis, the one-man-band heir to Riot's method. ~108 minutes.
