The world

The nocturne is the one piano form invented for the night itself. John Field — Irish, Clementi's pupil, settled in Russia — published the first pieces to carry the name in 1812: a singing right hand over a rocking left hand and a lifted pedal, music for a room after the guests have gone. Chopin took the template and made it a genre; the Mendelssohn–Schumann circle in Leipzig gave it a German name (Notturno, Nachtstück); by the 1890s Fauré, Grieg and the very old Liszt had turned it into twilight — harmony dissolving, the tune half-remembered. The twentieth century kept the room and lost the melody: Satie's late nocturnes are plain as prayer, Mompou called his silent music, Bill Evans improvised one at the end of a Riverside session, Pärt reduced it to a triad and a scale. What we now call "neo-classical piano" — Richter, Frahm, Rani, Ott's Jóhannsson — is simply the nocturne with the lights turned down one more notch.

Connective tissue & sequencing

  • The Field thread: Field's Nocturne No. 1 (1812) opens; Chopin's Op. 27 No. 2 follows immediately so the inheritance is audible — same rocking left hand, the song now bel canto; Barber's Nocturne "Homage to John Field" (1959) closes the loop 150 years later.
  • Alice Sara Ott holds both ends: she plays the first nocturne ever written (track 1, from her 2025 Field: Complete Nocturnes) and the last word (track 18, Jóhannsson's Theory of Everything on piano) — the pianist from Saturday's Sonos session bookending the form's whole history.
  • The German night: Fanny Mendelssohn's Notturno (1838) and Robert Schumann's Nachtstücke No. 4 (1839, the title borrowed from E.T.A. Hoffmann's night tales) — the salon Leipzig version of what Chopin was doing in Paris.
  • Twilight of the Romantics: Liszt's two-minute En rêve (1885, one of his last pieces), Grieg's Notturno with its nightingale trill, then Fauré's Sixth — the emotional summit of the whole set, placed at track 7 so everything after it is descent.
  • France → stillness: Debussy's moon-over-the-temple → Satie's Nocturne No. 1 (1919, his last piano works, serious and Chopin-haunted) → Bill Evans's Peace Piece, improvised in December 1958 over the vamp of Bernstein's Some Other Time — Evans loved Satie and Debussy, and it shows.
  • 1959, two cities: Mompou's Angelico (Barcelona, "silent music" after St John of the Cross) beside Barber's Field homage (New York) — the same year, the same hush.
  • The trough: Pärt's Für Alina (1976, the first tintinnabuli piece) in Malter's eleven-minute ECM version is the deepest point of the night, followed by Takemitsu's Les yeux clos (1979, in memory of the poet Shūzō Takiguchi, titled after Redon's painting) — Debussy's harmonies heard from Tokyo.
  • Small lamps: the 21st century arrives as miniatures — Richter's 79-second Vladimir's Blues (Nabokov's butterflies), Frahm's You from Screws (nine pieces for nine fingers, written at night after he broke his thumb), Rani's Glass from Esja — before Ott's Jóhannsson turns out the light.
  • Order is chronological on purpose: 1812 → 2025 in eighteen steps, so the form's evolution — song, dream, silence, lamp — is the shape of the hour.
