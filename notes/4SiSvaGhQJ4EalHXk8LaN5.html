The world

The unaccompanied choir is the oldest ensemble in Western music and the one that never stopped: the same room — a loft, a chancel, a rehearsal hall — has held Pérotin's organum at Notre Dame (St. Stephen's Day, c. 1199), Tallis writing plain English for the Edwardian church (c. 1547), Victoria and Monteverdi and Lotti in Latin for Rome, Venice and Dresden, the German men's-choir part-song of Schubert and Schumann and Mendelssohn, the national hymns and folk settings of Sibelius, Kodály and Tormis, and the American a cappella tradition of Thompson, Shaw and the college glee club that Cantus grew out of. On the Shoulders of Giants is Cantus's argument that this is one unbroken craft, and the playlist takes that programme as a book — the same 16 works, chronologically, each in another choir's reading, so the room changes (Birmingham, New York, Paris, London, Berlin, Leipzig, Munich, Helsinki, Debrecen, Atlanta, Tallinn) while the thing being done in it does not. Seven languages — Latin, English, German, Finnish, Hungarian, Estonian and Rahman's Arabic/Urdu zikr — and almost all of it slow.

Selection method

Frame (a) of the songbook shape — a performer's programme as the book, readings by others (the Legrand / Barron convention from Other Places and the door-artist convention from Sketch for Autumn): one reading per work, one work per composer, one recording per choir, with Cantus's own readings kept only where the arrangement is effectively theirs — the Pérotin excerpt that opens their album, Bernstein's "Somewhere", Ethan Sperry's men's-voice arrangement of Rahman's "Zikr" (from Bose: The Forgotten Hero, 2005) and the "MLK" arrangement — four door cuts, all liked, none ever played. The album's two-track Kaksikpühendus is one diptych (Tormis, 1983; texts Gustav Suits and Marie Under), so it is one work here, in the single-track Estonian reading — 17 album tracks → 16 works. Readings were chosen by lineage, not by Spotify's neighbours: the Estonian State Academic Male Choir (Olev Oja) is the choir Tormis wrote his men's-voice music for; YL Male Voice Choir + Helsinki University Choir (Matti Hyökki, BIS Complete Male Choir Works) carry the Finlandia hymn as a national text (Koskenniemi's words, 1940; Sibelius's own choral setting, 1948); the Debrecen Kodály Choir sings Kodály's 1938 Esti dal in Hungarian; the MDR Leipzig Radio Choir (Philipp Ahmann) sings "Hebe deine Augen auf" from Elias in Mendelssohn's own city and language; the Rundfunkchor Berlin men's choir (Dietrich Knothe) and Die Singphoniker (Munich) take the Schubert (D. 983c, Op. 17 No. 4, c. 1822) and Schumann (Ritornelle in canonischen Weisen Op. 65, Rückert, 1847) part-songs; Le Concert Spirituel (Hervé Niquet) the Monteverdi (SV 293, six voices, Bianchi's Libro primo de motetti, Venice 1620); Tenebrae (Nigel Short) the Lotti Crucifixus a 8 (c. 1718); the Choir of St. Ignatius Loyola (Kent Tritle, New York) the Victoria O vos omnes (1572); Ex Cathedra (Jeffrey Skidmore, Birmingham) the Tallis; the Robert Shaw Chamber Singers Thompson's Alleluia (written in five days in July 1940 for Koussevitzky, premiered at Tanglewood on July 8); and Anonymous 4 with Bruce Molsky's fiddle Foster's "Hard Times" (1854) from 1865, the one reading that is a vocal quartet rather than a choir. Connective tissue & sequencing

Chronological by composition, which is not how Cantus sequence theirs — they open with "Sederunt" and then move freely. It opens where the book opens — Cantus's own "Sederunt", the c. 1199 organum quadruplum one of the two surviving organa quadrupla (the full 12-minute Hilliard Ensemble reading on ECM's Perotin is the pointer if the excerpt isn't enough) — and runs the sacred Latin/English cluster (Tallis → Victoria → Monteverdi → Lotti, 1547–1718: the Lotti is the one everyone knows, the suspensions that every later choir learned from); then the German Romantic part-song cluster (Schubert's "Die Nacht" → Mendelssohn's angels → Schumann's canon), the only stretch where Cantus's low-voice identity is the actual genre — men's-choir music from the Liedertafel era; Foster's "Hard Times" is the hinge from Europe to America, and the only track with an instrument. The national cluster follows (Finland 1940, Hungary 1938, then Thompson's wordless American "Alleluia" of the same summer, 1940 — three countries' choral answers to the same year), then "Somewhere" as the one Broadway text, Tormis's diptych as the modern Baltic peak, and the close out of order on purpose: Rahman's "Zikr" is the loudest, fastest thing on the list — the one ecstatic track — placed second-to-last as the climax, so that U2's "MLK" (1984, The Unforgettable Fire: "sleep, sleep tonight, and may your dreams be realized") can close the book as the lullaby it is, in the arrangement Cantus sing on the album (where it sits sixth, not last). Energy stamped honestly as hushed: Sederunt, Finlandia, the Tormis and Zikr carry some weight, but an hour of a cappella choral music is a quiet hour.
